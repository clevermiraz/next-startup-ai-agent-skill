# Human Buying Psychology

## Purpose

Help an AI agent understand and respond to the psychological, practical, social, and contextual factors that shape buying decisions. Use this knowledge to help customers make informed decisions and help businesses communicate genuine value.

This skill is for ethical persuasion, not manipulation. The objective is a good-fit purchase and durable customer trust—not conversion at any cost.

## Operating principles

1. **Understand before persuading.** Discover the buyer's situation, desired outcome, constraints, alternatives, and decision process before recommending an offer.
2. **Treat psychology as probabilistic.** Behavioral findings describe tendencies, not universal laws. Never assume a person has a motive or bias merely because they belong to a demographic group.
3. **Connect features to outcomes.** Explain what a product does, why it matters to this buyer, and what evidence supports the claim.
4. **Reduce uncertainty, not autonomy.** Make comparison, pricing, risks, cancellation, and next steps clear. Do not pressure people into decisions.
5. **Use evidence honestly.** Never invent testimonials, customer counts, scarcity, deadlines, guarantees, performance data, or ROI.
6. **Respect context.** Buying behavior varies by category, stakes, experience, culture, financial circumstances, and purchase setting.
7. **Optimize for customer outcomes.** A lower-priced plan, a competitor, delaying a purchase, or not buying may be the right recommendation.
8. **Separate evidence from hypotheses.** Label observed facts, customer statements, interpretations, and assumptions distinctly.

## When to use this skill

Activate this skill when asked to:

- Understand why a customer or segment buys, hesitates, abandons, or churns.
- Conduct customer discovery, sales qualification, interviews, or win/loss analysis.
- Write sales emails, ads, landing pages, product descriptions, proposals, or presentations.
- Improve product positioning, value propositions, pricing pages, packaging, or checkout flows.
- Handle objections about price, trust, timing, risk, complexity, or alternatives.
- Design ethical experiments to improve conversion, activation, retention, or customer confidence.
- Compare B2B and B2C buying journeys or adapt messaging to a market.

Do not use this skill to exploit vulnerable people, create deceptive urgency, conceal material information, or bypass informed consent.

## 1. A practical model of buying

A purchase is rarely a single rational calculation. It can involve several interacting forces:

- **Functional:** Does this solve the problem or perform the job?
- **Economic:** Is the expected benefit worth the money, time, and switching cost?
- **Emotional:** Will the buyer feel safer, more confident, relieved, proud, excited, or less frustrated?
- **Social:** How might the decision affect reputation, belonging, status, or relationships?
- **Risk-related:** What could go wrong, and who bears the consequences?
- **Contextual:** Is the buyer ready, able, authorized, and in a situation where purchase is possible?

Do not reduce every purchase to emotion or every business purchase to ROI. Determine which forces matter in the specific case.

### Buying journey

Use this as a flexible map, not a rigid funnel:

1. **Trigger / need recognition:** A problem, goal, event, or desire makes the current situation feel insufficient.
2. **Exploration:** The buyer searches, asks peers, notices brands, or investigates possible approaches.
3. **Evaluation:** The buyer compares expected outcomes, price, risk, trust, effort, and alternatives.
4. **Decision:** The buyer chooses, delays, negotiates, seeks approval, or decides not to buy.
5. **Onboarding / consumption:** The buyer tests whether the promise matches reality.
6. **Post-purchase evaluation:** Satisfaction, regret, habit, support, and results influence retention, reviews, and referrals.

People can skip stages, repeat them, or return to research after receiving new information. Identify the buyer's current stage before selecting a response.

## 2. Discover the buyer's real motivation

Do not assume the first stated requirement is the underlying reason for buying. Ask open, neutral questions and listen for the buyer's own language.

### Discovery sequence

Ask only the questions needed for the situation; do not interrogate the buyer.

1. **Context:** "What prompted you to look into this now?"
2. **Current state:** "How are you handling this today?"
3. **Problem:** "What is most difficult or frustrating about the current approach?"
4. **Impact:** "What does that problem cost in time, money, quality, risk, or missed opportunity?"
5. **Desired outcome:** "If this worked well, what would be different?"
6. **Priority:** "Why is solving this important now?"
7. **Alternatives:** "What other options are you considering?"
8. **Decision criteria:** "What will matter most when you choose?"
9. **Constraints:** "Is there a budget, deadline, integration, approval, or other constraint?"
10. **Confidence:** "What would you need to see to feel comfortable moving forward?"

### Motivation categories

Use these as hypotheses to validate, not labels to impose:

| Motivation | What the buyer may value | Useful evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Save money | Lower total cost, predictable spend | Transparent cost comparison |
| Save time | Less manual work, faster completion | Measured time saved |
| Reduce risk | Reliability, compliance, support | Security details, uptime, references |
| Improve performance | Better output or results | Benchmarks, pilot, case study |
| Convenience | Simpler workflow, fewer steps | Product demonstration |
| Control | Visibility, customization, ownership | Clear settings and permissions |
| Belonging / social acceptance | Fit with peers or community | Relevant, genuine customer stories |
| Status / identity | Professional image or self-expression | Authentic brand and product experience |
| Relief / confidence | Less uncertainty or frustration | Clear process, support, guarantees |
| Growth / opportunity | New capability or upside | Credible scenarios and proof |

### Listen for buying signals

Potential signals include a buyer describing a specific use case, asking about implementation, involving stakeholders, comparing plans, or asking about next steps. These are clues, not proof of purchase intent. Confirm meaning rather than assuming.

## 3. Understand perception, attention, and cognitive effort

People have limited attention and may simplify complex choices. Make information easy to notice, understand, and verify.

- Lead with the buyer's relevant problem and outcome, not a long feature list.
- Use concrete language, short sections, descriptive headings, and clear comparisons.
- Put material terms where buyers can find them: price, limits, exclusions, renewal, cancellation, and risks.
- Use examples that match the buyer's situation.
- Avoid overwhelming the buyer with every feature at once.
- Provide deeper technical or financial detail when the stakes or buyer's involvement require it.

### Cognitive effort

A buyer may rely more on simple cues when the decision is routine, attention is limited, or information is overwhelming. For high-stakes or complex purchases, provide substantive evidence and time to evaluate. Do not mistake a quick decision for informed understanding.

## 4. Emotion and reasoning

Emotion can influence what people notice, value, and remember. Deliberate reasoning helps buyers evaluate claims, trade-offs, and consequences. Both can matter in the same decision.

A useful communication sequence:

1. Reflect the buyer's real situation.
2. Explain the relevant outcome.
3. Demonstrate how the product produces that outcome.
4. Provide evidence and disclose limitations.
5. Clarify costs, risks, and alternatives.
6. Invite questions and let the buyer decide.

Do not manufacture fear, shame, insecurity, or urgency. Avoid simplistic claims such as "people buy emotionally and justify logically" as a universal rule.

## 5. Behavioral effects: use carefully

These concepts can help explain observed behavior. They are not reliable levers that work on everyone.

### Anchoring

An initial number or comparison can influence later judgments.

**Responsible use:** Show a real reference price, baseline, or cost of the current process, and explain how it was calculated.

**Avoid:** Inflated list prices, fake discounts, or irrelevant comparisons.

### Loss aversion and status quo effects

People may weigh potential losses and the disruption of change heavily.

**Responsible use:** Explain credible costs of inaction and the risks of switching, alongside the benefits of change.

**Avoid:** Threatening language, exaggerated consequences, or implying certain loss without evidence.

### Social proof

Evidence that relevant peers have used a product can reduce uncertainty.

**Responsible use:** Use genuine reviews, verifiable case studies, and customer examples with permission. Show context, dates, and limitations where material.

**Avoid:** Fake reviews, fabricated user counts, undisclosed paid endorsements, or implying that popularity proves suitability.

### Scarcity and urgency

Limited availability or deadlines can affect decisions.

**Responsible use:** Communicate a real inventory limit, capacity constraint, or genuine offer end date with clear terms.

**Avoid:** Resetting countdown timers, fake "only 2 left" messages, false deadlines, or pressure that prevents evaluation.

### Framing

The way equivalent information is presented can affect interpretation.

**Responsible use:** Present benefits and relevant trade-offs in plain language. For important statistics, give the denominator, time period, and comparison.

**Avoid:** Cherry-picked percentages, hiding absolute numbers, or framing that materially misleads.

### Default effects

Preselected options can influence choices.

**Responsible use:** Choose customer-friendly defaults, explain them, and make changing or declining easy.

**Avoid:** Prechecked paid add-ons, hidden subscriptions, difficult cancellation, or consent by ambiguity.

### Choice overload

Too many poorly differentiated options can make comparison harder.

**Responsible use:** Offer a small, meaningfully differentiated set of options, explain who each fits, and provide a way to see full details.

**Avoid:** Hiding important options or artificially restricting comparison.

### Endowment and ownership effects

People may value something more after they feel ownership of it.

**Responsible use:** Offer a transparent trial or demo that helps buyers assess real fit.

**Avoid:** Making trial cancellation difficult or using confusing trial-to-paid transitions.

### Confirmation bias

People may favor information that supports what they already believe.

**Responsible use:** Ask what evidence would change the buyer's mind and present balanced evidence, including limitations.

**Avoid:** Reinforcing false beliefs just to win agreement.

### Important caution

Effect sizes and reliability vary by setting. Do not present a behavioral principle as a guaranteed conversion tactic. Test changes with appropriate controls and monitor customer outcomes, complaints, refunds, and retention—not only clicks or immediate sales.

## 6. Trust and perceived risk

Trust is not created by persuasive language alone. It is earned when claims, product behavior, policies, and customer experience are consistent.

Buyers may worry about:

- Product performance and reliability.
- Financial loss or hidden fees.
- Data privacy and security.
- Implementation effort and disruption.
- Vendor stability and support quality.
- Social or professional consequences if the decision fails.
- Lock-in, cancellation, migration, and reversibility.

### Trust-building checklist

- State who provides the product and how to contact them.
- Explain what is included and excluded.
- Show accurate pricing and billing terms.
- Provide verifiable proof relevant to the buyer.
- Explain data collection, use, storage, and deletion in accessible language.
- Describe implementation, support, refunds, cancellation, and limitations.
- Admit what is unknown.
- Avoid guarantees that cannot be honored.
- Make it easy to ask questions or decline.

Trust research often finds a positive relationship between trust and purchase intention, but the strength and causes vary across contexts. Treat trust as a practical requirement, not a slogan.

## 7. Value perception and pricing

Buyers compare the price with the value they expect, the alternatives available, the risk they perceive, and their ability to pay.

### Communicate value

Use this structure:

**Capability → practical benefit → measurable outcome → evidence → limitation**

Example:

- Capability: "Automatically generates a weekly report."
- Benefit: "Your team spends less time compiling updates."
- Outcome: "The team can spend more time reviewing performance."
- Evidence: "In a measured pilot, the process took 45 minutes less per week."
- Limitation: "The result depends on data quality and setup."

Only use numbers when they are supported by real data. Label estimates and assumptions.

### Pricing presentation

- State the billing period, currency, taxes, renewal, and usage limits.
- Explain the differences between plans in terms of customer needs.
- Make the total cost of ownership visible where relevant.
- Explain setup, migration, support, and overage fees.
- Use reference prices only when genuine and defensible.
- Offer a lower-commitment path when commercially feasible.
- Avoid disguising recurring charges as one-time fees.

### ROI calculation

When relevant, calculate a conservative scenario:

`Net value = quantified benefits - total costs`

`ROI (%) = (net value / total costs) × 100`

Include the period, assumptions, implementation cost, ongoing cost, and uncertainty. Do not count speculative benefits as guaranteed savings.

## 8. Social influence and customer evidence

Use the most relevant proof, not simply the largest number.

Prioritize:

1. Evidence from customers with similar needs and constraints.
2. Independently verifiable outcomes.
3. Specific testimonials with permission.
4. Transparent product demonstrations.
5. Third-party reviews and certifications that are current and applicable.

When presenting a case study, include the customer's starting point, intervention, measurement period, result, and relevant caveats. Disclose incentives or material relationships.

## 9. B2C and B2B buying contexts

### B2C

Often involves an individual or household, shorter journeys for routine purchases, and personal preferences. However, high-value consumer decisions can involve extensive research and multiple people.

Consider:
- Ease of use and convenience.
- Personal goals and preferences.
- Budget and affordability.
- Reviews, returns, delivery, and support.
- Product fit and post-purchase experience.

### B2B

Often involves multiple stakeholders, organizational constraints, and a longer evaluation process. Identify the people who use, evaluate, approve, pay for, and may block the purchase.

Consider:
- Business problem and measurable impact.
- Technical fit, security, compliance, and integration.
- Procurement, budget ownership, and approval process.
- Implementation effort and change management.
- Vendor risk, support, and long-term cost.
- Evidence each stakeholder needs.

Do not assume B2B is purely rational or B2C is purely emotional. Both involve human judgment, incentives, relationships, and risk.

## 10. Ethical sales and objection handling

Treat objections as information. Do not treat every objection as something to defeat.

Use this sequence:

1. **Acknowledge:** "That makes sense."
2. **Clarify:** "Is the main concern the total cost, or whether the return is predictable?"
3. **Understand:** "What would make that concern manageable?"
4. **Respond:** Give relevant facts, options, or evidence.
5. **Check:** "Does that address the concern, or is something still missing?"
6. **Respect:** Accept that the buyer may decide not to proceed.

### Common objections

**"It's too expensive."**
- Ask what budget or alternative they are comparing against.
- Clarify whether the issue is affordability, uncertain value, or timing.
- Explain total cost and relevant outcomes.
- Offer a smaller scope or lower-cost fit if available.
- Do not shame the buyer or fabricate ROI.

**"I need to think about it."**
- Ask whether any information is missing.
- Offer a concise summary of benefits, costs, risks, and next steps.
- Agree on a follow-up only if the buyer wants one.
- Do not invent a deadline.

**"We already use another solution."**
- Ask what works well and what remains unresolved.
- Compare only relevant capabilities and costs.
- Acknowledge when switching is not worthwhile.

**"I don't trust this company/product yet."**
- Ask what proof or risk is most important.
- Provide verifiable references, documentation, trial options, or clear policies.
- Never pressure the buyer to ignore a legitimate concern.

**"Now is not the right time."**
- Understand the timing constraint.
- Determine whether there is a real event or dependency.
- Offer useful information or a buyer-chosen follow-up.
- Accept "not now" as a valid answer.

## 11. Agent workflow

Follow this workflow when performing a buying-psychology task.

### Step 1: Identify the task

Determine whether the user needs customer research, sales support, marketing copy, pricing advice, product positioning, objection handling, or conversion analysis.

### Step 2: Establish context

Collect only relevant details:
- Product or service and category.
- Target buyer and use case.
- Market and purchase context.
- Price range and alternatives.
- Customer evidence available.
- Desired business outcome.
- Legal, brand, and ethical constraints.

If a critical detail is missing, ask one focused clarification. Otherwise state reasonable assumptions.

### Step 3: Separate evidence from assumptions

Create three categories:
- **Known:** Directly supplied or verified facts.
- **Hypothesis:** Plausible interpretation to test.
- **Unknown:** Information not yet available.

Do not invent customer motivations, research findings, market data, or product capabilities.

### Step 4: Map the buying situation

Identify:
- Trigger and current alternative.
- Functional and emotional desired outcomes.
- Main decision criteria.
- Perceived risks and objections.
- Stakeholders and approval process.
- Stage of the buying journey.
- Constraints and switching costs.

### Step 5: Choose the relevant principles

Use only concepts that explain the actual situation. Do not force every bias or framework into every response.

### Step 6: Develop a customer-centered recommendation

Provide:
- The buyer's likely priority, with uncertainty clearly labeled.
- The relevant value proposition.
- Evidence that supports the claim.
- Trade-offs, limitations, and risks.
- A suitable next step that preserves choice.

### Step 7: Produce the requested output

Match the user's requested format: strategy, questions, sales script, landing-page copy, email, pricing analysis, experiment plan, or report.

### Step 8: Review for ethics and accuracy

Before finalizing, check:
- Is every factual claim supported?
- Are estimates labeled?
- Are price and terms clear?
- Is urgency genuine?
- Is social proof authentic?
- Can the customer decline or compare alternatives?
- Does the recommendation fit the customer's stated needs?
- Would the approach still be acceptable if the buyer understood exactly how it works?

## 13. Conversion optimization and measurement

Measure whether the experience helps the right customers make good decisions.

Potential metrics:
- Qualified conversion rate.
- Checkout or proposal completion.
- Time to decision, interpreted with context.
- Trial activation and time to first value.
- Refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks.
- Retention and repeat purchase.
- Customer satisfaction and complaint themes.
- Sales cycle and implementation success.
- Mis-selling, support burden, and expectation mismatch.

Use controlled A/B tests when practical. Change one meaningful variable at a time where possible, define the primary metric in advance, and avoid declaring a winner from tiny or biased samples.

A conversion increase is not automatically a success. If refunds, complaints, cancellations, or customer harm rise, investigate before adopting the change.

## 14. Common failure modes

- Assuming all buyers share the same motivations.
- Treating a psychological bias as a guaranteed tactic.
- Using generic personas instead of actual customer evidence.
- Listing features without explaining relevant outcomes.
- Overloading buyers with information.
- Using fake scarcity, fake reviews, or misleading discounts.
- Hiding limitations, recurring fees, or cancellation terms.
- Treating every objection as resistance to overcome.
- Confusing attention, clicks, or stated intent with actual value.
- Optimizing immediate conversion while damaging retention and trust.
- Making cultural stereotypes about individuals.
- Presenting correlation as proof of causation.

## 15. Quality checklist

Before delivering a recommendation or sales asset, verify:

- [ ] The target buyer and use case are clear.
- [ ] The buyer's stated problem is represented accurately.
- [ ] The message connects features to meaningful outcomes.
- [ ] Claims are supported, qualified, or clearly labeled as assumptions.
- [ ] Pricing, terms, risks, and limitations are transparent.
- [ ] Social proof and urgency are genuine.
- [ ] The buyer retains meaningful choice.
- [ ] The recommendation is appropriate for the buyer's constraints.
- [ ] The next step is specific and non-coercive.
- [ ] Success is evaluated beyond immediate conversion.

## 16. Research confidence and limitations

Use peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, reputable academic books, and official ethical or regulatory guidance where possible. Prefer primary research for specific claims.

When citing research:
- Identify the source and year.
- Describe the population and setting when known.
- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Do not generalize a narrow experiment to all buyers.
- Note when evidence is mixed, context-dependent, or contested.
- Verify current legal requirements for the relevant market.

The concepts in this skill are decision aids, not a diagnostic model of an individual. Ask the buyer, observe behavior, test hypotheses, and update conclusions.
